Buying land vs buying a house in The Gambia.

Real Estate 1 August 2026

It is the first question almost every buyer asks us, and the honest answer is that it depends on three things: when you need to live in it, how much risk you want to carry, and whether you will be in the country while the work happens.

Land: cheaper entry, longer road

A serviced plot is the lower number on the day you sign. It is also the start of a project rather than the end of one. You will need drawings, approvals, a contractor, a water supply, and someone on-site who can tell you honestly whether the blockwork went up correctly while you were 3,000 miles away.

Land makes sense when you have time, when you want a home built to your own brief rather than a standard specification, and when you have somebody you trust to supervise. It makes far less sense when you are buying from abroad with no representation on the ground.

The cost of land is the price of the plot plus every decision you have not made yet.

A finished home: higher number, fewer unknowns

A completed or off-plan home from an established developer moves most of the risk onto the developer’s balance sheet. The specification is set, the timeline is contractual, the title work is done before handover, and the services — water, power, drainage, security — are part of the estate rather than your problem to solve.

You are buying three things you cannot easily buy with a plot:

  • Certainty of cost. The price is the price. Construction inflation is the developer’s exposure, not yours.
  • Certainty of date. A construction-linked payment schedule means your instalments track visible progress.
  • Certainty of title. A registered 99-year or 50-year leasehold, completed before you take the keys.

What this looks like in numbers

Take a three-bedroom home. Buying land and building it yourself will usually land below the price of an equivalent finished apartment — if nothing goes wrong. Add eighteen months of supervision, currency movement on imported finishes, and the cost of a second trip to resolve a dispute, and the gap narrows quickly.

For a buyer living in The Gambia with time and local knowledge, land often wins. For a diaspora buyer who wants a rentable asset inside two years, a finished home almost always does.

Questions to ask either way

  1. Is the title registered, and can I see the instrument before I pay a deposit?
  2. What exactly is included in the price — and what is a later extra?
  3. Who supplies water and power, and what happens when they fail?
  4. Who manages the property after handover, and what does that cost per year?
  5. Can I walk a completed building by the same developer before I sign?

That last one matters more than any brochure. Standard is something you should be able to see and stand inside, not be told about.
